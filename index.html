01

Understanding the problem

Great digital products start with problems, not solutions. The most common failure in government tech isn't bad engineering — it's a team that built the wrong thing well.

Many projects fail not because of poor execution, but because they solve the wrong problem.Module 1

The tell-tale sign is a project that begins with a noun instead of a difficulty: "we need a CRM", "we should use AI". Both skip the diagnosis. Before naming a tool, you have to name the obstacle standing between people and the outcome you want.

Three core principlesthe posture behind every later framework
Problems before solutions
Diagnose what's actually blocking the outcome before you choose what to build.
Outcomes over outputs
An output is what you shipped. An outcome is what changed for people because you shipped it. Success is defined by change, not by features delivered.
Integrate policy, operations and technology
A digital fix rarely works alone. The policy rule, the day-to-day process and the system have to move together.

Start narrow. Grab began with taxis. Amazon began with books. Netflix began with DVDs by post. None of them started with the full vision — each nailed one problem first, then scaled.

In plain terms

If you can't say who is struggling, with what, and how you know — you are not ready to build. "Launched the portal" is an output. "Applications now take 4 minutes instead of 40" is an outcome.

02

Start with the whys

Before reaching for a solution, understand why the problem exists. The Five Whys is a simple tool that moves you from symptom to cause: state the problem, ask why it happens, then ask why of that answer, roughly five times over.

  1. SymptomCitizens submit the same personal details to three different agencies.
  2. Why?Each agency's form collects its own copy of the data.
  3. Why?No agency can read the data another already holds.
  4. Why?The systems were built separately, with no shared data layer.
  5. Why?There was no common data-sharing standard or mandate when they were built.
  6. Root cause — and the level you can act onData-sharing arrangements, not the form's design.

Note what happens to the solution as you descend the chain. At the top, you'd redesign a form. At the bottom, you'd integrate data — a completely different piece of work, aimed at a cause rather than a symptom.

Problems rarely have one root cause. The goal is to find causes that are within your control and closely connected to the outcome you want to change.Module 2

In plain terms

Five is a rule of thumb, not a quota. Stop when you hit a cause you can actually do something about. A "root cause" you have no authority to touch is interesting but useless; a cause that is in your control but barely affects the outcome is a distraction.

03

Craft a clear problem statement

Once you've found the cause, write it down in a form the whole team can agree on. The 4Cs gives a problem statement its four required parts — and, usefully, exposes which part you're missing.

The 4Csa problem statement is incomplete without all four
Clarity — what exactly is the problem?
Stated distinctly, for a specific group of people. No jargon, no solution smuggled inside.
Consequence — so what?
The impact if nothing changes: wasted time, cost, frustration, low take-up, risk.
Cause — why does it happen?
The underlying reason you surfaced with the Five Whys, not the surface symptom.
Confirmation — how do you know?
The evidence: user research, analytics, case volumes, service data. This is the C that turns an opinion into a problem statement.

Worked through on the recurring government example — citizens repeatedly filling the same forms across services:

The CApplied
ClarityCitizens encounter duplicate form requirements across government services.
ConsequenceTime wasted, frustration, and reduced service adoption.
CauseSystems lack data integration across agencies.
ConfirmationUser research and analytics verify the issue.
In plain terms

Read your draft back and ask which of the four is thinnest. Most first drafts are strong on Clarity and empty on Confirmation — which means the "problem" is still a hunch. And if your statement contains the words "we need a…", you've written a solution, not a problem.

04

Metrics that reflect real outcomes

A problem statement tells you what to change. Metrics tell you whether it changed. Three tools do the work here.

SMARTthe shape of a usable metric
Specific
Clearly defined — one thing, unambiguously.
Measurable
Quantifiable with data you can actually get.
Achievable
Realistic to obtain, not aspirational fiction.
Relevant
Connected to the objective in your problem statement.
Time-bound
Fixed to a defined timeframe.
Leading & lagging indicatorsused together, never alone
Leading indicators
Give early feedback on activities and processes — sign-ups this week, form completion rate, drop-off at step 3. They move fast and let you steer while there's still time.
Lagging indicators
Measure the real outcome after the fact — processing time reduced, cost per case, service adoption. They confirm impact but arrive too late to correct course.
Value-Cost Ratio (VCR)the funding conversation
What it measures
How much value is generated for every dollar spent.
Why it matters
It's how you make the case for continued investment — and how you compare "improve value" against "reduce cost" as two routes to the same better ratio.
In plain terms

Chain them: a leading indicator you can watch weekly should plausibly cause the lagging outcome you promised. If it doesn't, you're measuring activity, not impact — the metric equivalent of counting outputs.

05

Assumptions and risks

Every plan rests on things you believe but haven't checked. The discipline is to surface them early and ask two questions: what could go wrong, and what's the cheapest way to find out?

Three types of riskeach has its own cheap test
Market risk — will anyone use it?
De-risk by getting your product in front of real users as early as possible.
Technical risk — can it be built?
De-risk with small proof-of-concept work. At this stage, testability matters more than scalability.
Team risk — can this group deliver it?
De-risk by structuring for speed, focus and flexibility: policy, operations and technical expertise in one room, with clear accountability across every role.
STAGE 1Proof of ConceptDoes the approach work at all?
STAGE 2Proof of ValueDoes it produce measurable benefit?
STAGE 3ScaleExpand to larger user bases.
STAGE 4MaturitySustained, optimised operations.

The staged sequence is what lets you test assumptions before committing serious resources — the alternative to big-bang delivery.

The worked case. A health appointment booking service was first launched as a FormSG form feeding into an Excel sheet. Not scalable — deliberately so. It was the lowest-cost way to validate the approach. An A/B test then changed a single variable: the control group produced zero bookings, the treatment group produced 24, and users shared the link unprompted. That is a validated assumption for the price of a form.

In plain terms

An ugly thing that answers your riskiest question this week beats a polished thing that answers it next year. Ask "what's the cheapest way to find out?" before "how do we build this properly?"

06

A good customer experience

Good customer experience isn't a nice-to-have, it's necessary. This is especially true when services are compulsory, because citizens don't have the option to go elsewhere.Module 6

That's the argument that makes CX a government concern specifically. In a market, a bad experience shows up as lost customers, and the business fixes it to survive. When a service is mandatory, there is no exit — the bad experience just accumulates as unmeasured public frustration. Nobody churns, so nothing forces the fix. You have to hold the standard yourself.

The best government digital products do something specific: they remove a real, named frustration that users were experiencing before. ParkingSG removed paper coupons you had to tear and display — and the fine you got for mis-tearing them. RedeemSG removed the physical voucher you had to collect, carry and not lose. Both are narrow. Both are unmistakable.

The 11-star frameworka tool for recalibrating ambition
The exercise
Describe a 5-star experience — the adequate version. Then keep going: 6, 7, 8… up to an 11-star experience that is absurdly, impossibly good.
Why bother
Somewhere between adequate and impossible lies an experience that's genuinely worth building. The absurd end of the scale is where you find the ideas that a "realistic" brainstorm would have filtered out at birth.
In plain terms

Design for users, target one specific frustration, and make trade-offs on purpose rather than by accident. The 11-star exercise isn't a plan — it's a way of raising the ceiling before you start negotiating down.

07

Key takeaways

Product thinking is a discipline of asking better questions before reaching for solutions. It isn't a one-time methodology you run at kick-off — it's a way of working: a set of habits for diagnosing problems, making decisions, and learning from what you build.

ToolThe question it answers
5 WhysWhy does this problem actually exist?
4CsCan we all state the problem the same way?
SMART + VCRHow will we know it worked, and was it worth it?
Staged deliveryWhat's the cheapest way to find out we're wrong?
11-starHow good could this actually be?

The next step is not another course. Take an initiative already underway and run these frameworks over it — that's where product thinking becomes real.
